How to Read this Manuscript
Established evidence. Statements that should be supported by peer-reviewed literature, international recommendations,
standards or official documentation.
IML proposal. Concepts and methodological choices proposed by IML for discussion, testing and refinement.
Research hypothesis. Ideas that appear useful and plausible but require empirical validation.
Future work. Software modules, indicators, country extensions or scientific studies that remain to be completed.

Opening Statement
Health is humanity's common objective.
Progress in health information should not be measured by technological sophistication alone, but by its capacity to help
people make better decisions, reduce suffering and improve health together.
Every day, clinical observations, laboratory analyses, medical images, prescriptions, epidemiological reports, administrative
records and scientific discoveries contribute to our understanding of health. Yet information alone does not protect health. It
protects health only when it becomes trusted knowledge, available to the right person, at the right time, under the right
conditions.
The Interoperability Maturity Lab (IML) was created to examine that transformation. It proposes a scientific framework for
understanding, assessing and progressively improving Health Information Ecosystems, while also developing a practical
open-source implementation bridge through the Open Clinical Workspace.
This edition records an important transition. IML is no longer only a conceptual framework. The reference software
workstream has begun: PostgreSQL-based clinical structures, terminology canonicalisation, country adaptation logic and a
traceable AI evidence-retrieval scaffold now provide concrete objects that can be tested, criticised and improved. None of
these developments is presented as a validated clinical service.

Preface
Healthcare has entered an unprecedented digital era. Hospitals, primary care practices, laboratories, pharmacies, insurers,
public-health authorities and research institutions generate and exchange vast quantities of information. Yet fragmentation
persists, often at precisely the moment when continuity matters most.
The consequence is not merely inconvenience. Decisions may be made with incomplete information, diagnostic procedures
may be repeated, prevention opportunities may be missed, and public-health surveillance may identify resistant organisms
without knowing whether they represent symptomatic infection, colonisation, asymptomatic carriage or contamination.
IML does not seek to replace the achievements of WHO, HL7 International, SNOMED International, openEHR, LOINC,
IHE, ISO, national health authorities or the Global Digital Health Monitor. Its contribution is narrower and more clinically
oriented: to examine whether digital capacity preserves meaning, context, trust and usefulness across the ecosystem.
The framework therefore combines assessment with action. Country profiles should show evidence and limitations rather
than produce simplistic rankings. The Open Clinical Workspace should provide a reference implementation environment in
which the principles of the manuscript can be tested without creating a new proprietary dependency.
Artificial intelligence is treated in the same way. IML neither rejects AI nor treats it as self-validating. AI becomes useful
only when the information ecosystem is sufficiently trustworthy, when provenance is preserved, when limitations are visible
and when human responsibility remains explicit.

The IML Declaration
Health is one of humanity's greatest shared responsibilities. Trustworthy information is an enabling condition for
prevention, diagnosis, treatment, rehabilitation, research and public health.

IML is an independent scientific initiative dedicated to understanding, assessing and progressively improving Health
Information Ecosystems. It does not promote a particular software vendor, national model or commercial platform. It
supports open standards, inspectable structures, evidence-based evaluation, reproducibility and progressive implementation.
Interoperability is not an objective in itself. Its value lies in preserving meaning and enabling the safe use of information
across boundaries. Technical exchange is necessary but insufficient; sustainable interoperability also depends on
governance, institutional engagement, professional trust, security, legal certainty, correction and learning.
Artificial intelligence is recognised as an ecosystem actor and as an instrument that may assist professionals, researchers
and institutions. Scientific judgement, ethical responsibility and clinical decisions remain human responsibilities. AI outputs
should be attributable, reviewable and linked to the evidence and data on which they depend.
No country or institution can achieve interoperability alone. Every ecosystem can improve, and every documented failure
can become an opportunity to learn.

The IML Commitment
 • Put health before technology.
 • Promote trustworthy, contextualised and correctable health information.
 • Support continuity of information across care, public health, research and financing.
 • Use open standards and open-source reference implementations where they improve transparency and autonomy.
 • Assess ecosystems rather than isolated products.
 • Treat institutional responsiveness, correction and learning as observable aspects of maturity.
 • Use artificial intelligence proportionately, transparently and under human responsibility.
 • Support country-specific adaptation without fragmenting the common architecture.
 • Prefer progressive improvement and evidence profiles over competitive country rankings.

PART 0 · Why This White Paper Exists
0.1 Why another white paper?
Digital health has achieved extraordinary technical progress, yet the existence of digital systems does not guarantee
continuity of usable information. The recurring problem is the gap between data that exist and knowledge that can actually
support a decision.
IML therefore focuses on transformation rather than digitalisation. It asks whether information remains meaningful across
organisational and technical boundaries, whether errors can be corrected, whether institutions can learn and whether the
ecosystem can support care when infrastructures are constrained.

0.2 A changing paradigm
The first digital-health paradigm was computerisation. The second was exchange. A third is now emerging: information
must improve decisions. This changes the unit of analysis from the application to the information ecosystem and from
message transfer to preservation of meaning.
Technology becomes an instrument. Health remains the objective.

0.3 Why Health Information Ecosystems?
Patients move across organisations, professionals collaborate, samples circulate, public-health agencies aggregate data,
payers hold administrative information and scientific knowledge changes over time. These relationships form an ecosystem
rather than a collection of independent systems.
IML therefore evaluates actors, flows, responsibilities, interfaces, context, correction and learning together.

0.4 Beyond technical interoperability
Technical interoperability is essential, but an interface can work while clinical meaning is lost, responsibility is unclear or
institutions fail to respond to known defects. IML expands interoperability to include semantic, organisational, institutional,
clinical and public-health dimensions.
Successful interoperability depends on standards, governance, implementation capacity, trust, professional practice and
durable institutional follow-through.

0.5 Complementarity rather than duplication
IML is complementary to WHO, the Global Digital Health Monitor, national digital-health strategies and existing maturity
models. Country-level digital capacity remains valuable context, but IML adds an evidence-oriented and clinically grounded
question: does that capacity preserve meaning, context, trust and usefulness in real workflows?
Where official information is incomplete or unvalidated, IML records uncertainty rather than filling gaps with assumption.

PART I · Health Before Technology
1.1 Health as the objective
IML uses a broad health perspective consistent with the long-standing WHO view of health as more than the absence of
disease. Digital systems should therefore be judged by their contribution to physical, mental and social well-being,
prevention, continuity and equity.
A clinically sophisticated application that increases fragmentation or prevents correction may be technologically impressive
while remaining poorly aligned with health.

1.2 Information as an enabling determinant of health
Health information shapes access to diagnosis, treatment, prevention and coordination. Missing or misleading information
can therefore act as a practical determinant of health outcomes.
This does not mean that information replaces social, biological or environmental determinants. It means that trustworthy
information can amplify the effectiveness of many health interventions.

1.3 Continuity of health information
Continuity of care depends on continuity of information. The relevant concept is not unrestricted centralisation but the
ability to recover the right information with preserved context and provenance when a legitimate clinical, public-health or
research purpose arises.
Continuity should survive changes in provider, software, institution and, where lawful, country.

1.4 Prevention and public health
Interoperable information can strengthen screening, vaccination continuity, medication safety, chronic-disease follow-up
and outbreak detection. Yet prevention requires context. A code without timing, severity, source and outcome may be
insufficient for action.
IML therefore treats prevention as a test of whether the ecosystem transforms scattered observations into actionable
knowledge.

1.5 Equity and constrained environments
Interoperability must not become a privilege of well-funded institutions. Low-resource settings often require simpler
infrastructure, offline-capable workflows and progressive deployment rather than large national platforms.
The Open Clinical Workspace is designed with this constraint in mind: useful first installations should be possible without
purchasing an entire digital-health ecosystem.

1.6 Trust and correction
Trust is not produced by branding or by a single security control. It emerges from provenance, accountability, correction,
understandable governance and the ability to explain what is known and what remains uncertain.
An ecosystem that cannot correct errors or propagate corrections safely cannot sustain trust over time.

PART II · Health Information Ecosystems
2.1 Definition
A Health Information Ecosystem is the governed network of people, institutions, technologies, standards, data flows and
responsibilities through which health information is produced, transformed, exchanged, interpreted, corrected and reused.
The definition intentionally includes both technical and human actors. A laboratory interface, a clinician, a payer, a
terminology authority and an AI service may all influence the meaning and reliability of information.

2.2 Actors and relationships
Core actors include patients, clinicians, laboratories, pharmacies, hospitals, primary care, public-health bodies, research
institutions, public and private payers, regulators, software suppliers, standards organisations and civil society.
Interoperability maturity depends not only on the strength of each actor but on the quality of the relationships between them.

2.3 Information lifecycle
IML follows information from creation through validation, storage, exchange, interpretation, correction, reuse and eventual
archival or deletion. Failures can occur at every stage.
A useful maturity assessment therefore asks not only whether data are exchanged, but whether provenance, versioning and
purpose remain visible through the lifecycle.

2.4 Clinical context preservation
A laboratory value or diagnostic code acquires clinical meaning through context: who, when, why, under which conditions,
and with what subsequent action and outcome. Context loss is therefore an interoperability failure even when the technical
payload is syntactically valid.
This principle is central to the AMR demonstrator and to the clinical data model of the Open Clinical Workspace.

2.5 Payer interoperability
Payers hold information about eligibility, reimbursement, prior treatment, service use and, in some systems, longitudinal
care pathways. When governance is appropriate, these data can contribute to continuity, prevention and evaluation.
IML treats payer interoperability as cross-cutting because the form of the payer varies across national health services, social
insurance, mutual insurance and private markets.

2.6 Professional role alignment
Health-information systems often fail when responsibilities encoded in software do not match real professional practice.
Role alignment therefore belongs to ecosystem maturity: clinicians, pharmacists, laboratory professionals, administrators
and patients must know what each can see, correct, approve and transmit.
Identity and access mechanisms should be purpose-limited and attributable rather than relying only on broad organisational
permission.

2.7 AI as an ecosystem actor
AI increasingly influences how information is searched, summarised, prioritised and interpreted. For this reason it should be
modelled as an ecosystem actor with explicit inputs, outputs, dependencies, provenance and accountability.
The durable record must not disappear when an AI provider changes. Models should be replaceable; evidence and clinical
provenance should persist.

PART III · Understanding Interoperability
3.1 Interoperability as preservation of meaning
Interoperability is the capacity of independently governed actors and systems to use information while preserving its
intended meaning, context, trust and accountability.
A successful message transfer is only the first layer.

3.2 Technical interoperability
Technical interoperability concerns connectivity, protocols, APIs, files, messaging, transport security, availability and the
ability of systems to exchange structured information reliably.
IML remains technology-neutral and allows progressive use of mature methods, from structured files to APIs and event-
driven exchange.

3.3 Semantic interoperability
Semantic interoperability requires shared concepts, codes, units and mappings. Terminology should be versioned,
attributable and reviewable.
The current software workstream treats terminology as a canonical service, including KSH97-P, ICD-10 and progressive
mapping logic rather than hidden application dictionaries.

3.4 Organisational interoperability
Organisations must align workflow, responsibility and service boundaries. A technically perfect API cannot compensate for
a process in which no actor is responsible for reviewing or correcting the incoming information.

3.5 Institutional interoperability
Institutions must be able to coordinate, respond to documented problems and act on authoritative findings. Formal contact
points are useful, but operational answerability requires substantive response and follow-through.

3.6 Clinical interoperability
Clinical interoperability is demonstrated when information can safely support clinical judgement. It requires context,
provenance, timeliness and an understanding of uncertainty.

Clinical interoperability is therefore stricter than syntactic exchange.

3.7 Public-health interoperability
Public-health interoperability connects individual observations with surveillance and population-level learning without
erasing the distinction between clinical phenomena and administrative or microbiological proxies.

3.8 Limits and safeguards
Mature interoperability includes the ability to refuse unnecessary exchange, limit purpose, preserve privacy, document
uncertainty and separate operational systems from research or AI experimentation.
More exchange is not automatically better interoperability.

3.9 Relationship to pre-existing interoperability maturity models
The WHO–ITU National eHealth Strategy Toolkit (2012), the MEASURE Evaluation HIS Interoperability Maturity Toolkit
(version 1.0, 2019), and the WHO Global Digital Health Monitor provide established reference points for policy,
governance, workforce, infrastructure, and standards. Their prior contributions must be acknowledged when assessing
originality of IML constructs.
IML proposes a complementary emphasis on preservation of clinical context, pathway-level evidence, institutional
documentary reliability, substantive answerability, and traceable correction. These are proposed differentiators, not
empirically validated superiority claims. See Annex K and the companion Comparative Literature Review (16 November
2026).

PART IV · The IML Framework
The framework translates ecosystem principles into six assessment domains. Cross-cutting dimensions include Institutional
Engagement, Payer Interoperability and AI Readiness. The framework is intended to produce evidence profiles, not
competitive rankings.

4.1 Domain 1 · Governance and Standards
Strategy, governance, legal frameworks, standards adoption, accountability and the capacity to convert policy into sustained
implementation.
4.1.1· Governance of workforce training in health-information interoperability
Definition (proposed): Whether responsible public authorities and healthcare organisations establish, finance, maintain and
evaluate role-appropriate training for healthcare staff in the safe and clinically meaningful use of health information systems
and interoperable workflows.
Assessment questions: (1) Is a responsible body designated? (2) Are training requirements and competencies formally
defined for clinical and non-clinical roles? (3) Are protected time, funding and equitable access provided? (4) Are curricula
updated with standards, incidents, user feedback and clinical changes? (5) Is the programme independently reviewed and
corrected?
Evidence: published policy and role assignments; budgets and training plans; curriculum versions; records of delivery and
access across professional groups; independent programme reviews and documented improvement. These sources establish
governance capacity, not necessarily staff proficiency or patient benefit.
Status: proposed addition for external methodological review, not scored in existing IML country profiles. It complements
MEASURE Evaluation Human Resources Policy and Human Resources Capacity Development; it does not create a seventh
IML domain.

4.2 Domain 2 · Technical Interoperability
Interfaces, APIs, messaging, structured exchange, availability, portability and infrastructure needed for reliable information
flow.

4.3 Domain 3 · Identity, Consent and Trust
Patient and professional identity, authentication, authorisation, consent or lawful basis, provenance and accountability.

4.4 Domain 4 · Adoption and Use
Actual use in clinical and organisational workflows, not merely formal availability.
4.4.1 · Demonstrated workforce competence
Verify whether professionals can actually retrieve, interpret, communicate and correct health information during
representative workflows. Evidence may include role-specific competency assessments, observed workflow tests,
documented support needs, refresher training and improvement after incidents. Attendance counts alone cannot demonstrate
competence. ADP-TRN-01 remains exploratory and unscored pending external validation.

4.5 Domain 5 · Security and Resilience
Cybersecurity, continuity, backup, recovery, incident response, lifecycle support and the ability to continue essential care.

4.6 Domain 6 · Feedback, Correction and Learning
A mature ecosystem does not merely exchange information; it detects errors, corrects them, learns from them and improves
its institutions and software over time. Learning therefore covers technical correction, documentary reliability and
institutional answerability.

LRN-5 belongs inside the Learning domain. It does not create a seventh domain and does not replace Institutional

For LRN-5, authoritative findings by courts of audit, parliamentary bodies, regulators, ombuds institutions or equivalent

Persistent institutional silence or inaction may be described by its functional effect as passive institutional obstruction when
the evidence supports that interpretation. This language should not be used to impute personal intent.

4.7 Cross-cutting dimension · Institutional Engagement
Institutional Engagement examines whether responsible organisations expose contact pathways, participate in review,
support pilots and engage with external scientific or implementation work.
INS-9 may assess the existence of procedures and reasonable response pathways; LRN-5 separately assesses actual
answerability and follow-through.

4.8 Cross-cutting dimension · Payer Interoperability
Payer-held information may contribute to prevention, continuity and evaluation, but its use must be proportionate and
governed. The relevant model varies by country and must not assume a single insurance architecture.

4.9 Cross-cutting dimension · AI Readiness
AI readiness depends on data quality, contextual integrity, governance, auditability, human oversight, security and the
capacity to identify model limitations. AI capability without these foundations should not be interpreted as maturity.

4.10 Crosswalk to the MEASURE Evaluation HIS interoperability maturity model
MEASURE Evaluation (2019) structures its HIS interoperability assessment around 18 subdomains in three domains
(leadership and governance, human resources, and technology), each characterized through five maturity levels. IML retains
six assessment domains and cross-cutting institutional, payer, and AI-readiness dimensions. The relationship is many-to-
many, not a conversion of scoring scales.
Annex K records the 18-item analytical crosswalk. It is for construct comparison and external review only; neither
MEASURE maturity levels nor IML scores should be inferred from the crosswalk.

PART V · Methodology
5.1 Evidence-oriented assessment
IML assessments should be built from traceable sources with explicit evidence levels, dates and limitations. Official
documentation is valuable but should not be confused with independent confirmation of implementation.
Evidence should be preserved so that another reviewer can understand how a conclusion was reached.

5.2 Country profiles rather than rankings
Profiles describe strengths, gaps, uncertainty and the evidence supporting each domain. A single overall number may help
orientation but should not erase the six-domain shape.
Scores should therefore remain accompanied by domain-level explanations and source registers.

5.3 Source quality and contradiction
Sources can be current yet incomplete, official yet aspirational, or technically accurate while clinically uninformative. IML
therefore records source type, authority, recency, scope and contradiction.
Where reliable sources disagree, the disagreement is part of the evidence profile.

5.4 Scoring philosophy
Scoring should reflect demonstrated capability rather than promises. The highest maturity levels require learning,
correction, resilience and institutional follow-through, not merely formal infrastructure.
Scoring rules should be versioned, auditable and open to external review.

5.5 Institutional answerability scale
LRN-5 may use a practical 0–100 interpretation: rapid substantive response with traceable corrective follow-through at the
high end; delayed or incomplete but usable response in the middle; acknowledgement without substantive answer or
effective follow-through at the lower end; and persistent non-response or failure to act after reasonable opportunity at zero.
Automated acknowledgements are not substantive responses. Cases outside an institution's remit, with insufficient evidence
or without demonstrable receipt should be marked not assessed rather than scored negatively.

5.6 Versioning and reproducibility
Indicator definitions, scoring rules, source registers, country profiles and software schemas should be versioned. Scientific
reproducibility includes the ability to reconstruct not only results but the evidence and transformation steps that produced
them.

5.7 Exploratory comparative workstream · Patient harm, redress and system learning
IML will examine a comparative patient-safety question at the intersection of rights, accountability and learning: when a
patient experiences harm associated with care, what happens next? The workstream will describe how health systems enable

explanation and review of the event, access to remedies or compensation, and the conversion of individual incidents into

The initial comparison will examine the following dimensions without presuming that one national model is superior:
 • Accessibility of the remedy or redress pathway, including cost, procedural complexity and whether legal representation
   is required.
 • Burden of proof and causal standards, including the distinction between fault-based, avoidability-based and no-fault
   approaches.
 • Timeliness from notification or claim to investigation, decision and payment or other resolution.
 • Scope of redress, including financial compensation, rehabilitation, support, explanation and other forms of repair.
 • Disclosure, candour and practical access to the clinical record and investigation findings.
 • Independence of assessment, expert review and availability of appeal or reconsideration.
 • Equity of access, including whether socioeconomic position materially affects the ability to obtain review or redress.
 • Learning loop, including whether incidents, complaints or claims are systematically converted into patient-safety
   improvement and recurrence prevention.
Evidence policy. The first phase will privilege official legal and administrative documentation, patient-safety authorities and
peer-reviewed literature. Legal entitlement will be distinguished from operational accessibility. High reporting or
compensation volumes will not automatically be interpreted as poor quality because they may also reflect transparency,
detection or easier access to redress. Where evidence is incomplete or incomparable, the limitation will be stated explicitly.
The WHO Patient Safety Rights Charter provides an initial international rights frame for the study. National compensation,
complaint, regulatory and patient-safety systems will be mapped as source classes, but inclusion in the map does not imply a
maturity judgement. Comparative interpretation will remain descriptive until the protocol has undergone review.

5.8 Comparative validation protocol (proposed, not yet validated)
Training indicator validation safeguard: compare GOV-TRN-01 (institutional programme and commitments) with ADP-
TRN-01 (demonstrated practical competence) in identical care settings. Document contextual differences, role mix,
evidence gaps, agreement between reviewers and observed workflow or safety outcomes. Do not infer effectiveness from a
policy, certificate or attendance list alone.
Before claiming added discriminative value, compare applicable MEASURE indicators and IML evidence criteria on the
same precisely bounded trace case. Preserve each instrument’s original unit of analysis; where a national indicator cannot
validly be evaluated using a local case, mark it not assessable rather than assigning a maturity level.
The protocol should preregister questions, map claims to dated sources, separate documented structures from operational
follow-through, independently review uncertain judgments, and compare agreement, disagreement, missing evidence and
feasibility. Initial candidates include a biology-laboratory signalment pathway and the long-term governance/evaluation
chain of the French DMP and Mon espace santé. These are candidates, not completed empirical evaluations.

PART VI · Demonstrator: Antimicrobial Resistance and Multidrug-
Resistant Bacteria
6.1 Why AMR/BMR?
Antimicrobial resistance is important enough to justify action, concrete enough to support implementation and complex
enough to test the full IML logic. It links microbiology, clinical context, treatment, outcomes, surveillance and governance.
The first reference use case focuses on multidrug-resistant Escherichia coli urinary tract infection.

6.2 Resistant isolate is not the clinical outcome
A resistant organism detected in a laboratory is not automatically a symptomatic infection. The same result may represent
colonisation, asymptomatic bacteriuria, contamination or clinically meaningful disease.

Interoperability must therefore connect microbiology to symptoms, diagnosis, treatment and outcome.

6.3 Minimum clinical dataset
Useful AMR interpretation requires at least clinical presentation, risk context, urinalysis where relevant, culture, bacterial
count, susceptibility testing, final diagnosis, antimicrobial treatment and evolution.
The dataset should remain minimal enough to be implementable while rich enough to distinguish clinically meaningful
states.

6.4 AI and AMR
AI may support pattern recognition, missing-context detection, evidence retrieval or decision support, but it cannot
compensate for fragmented or misleading source data.
AMR therefore provides a demanding test of responsible AI: the algorithm should inherit the context, provenance and
uncertainty of the underlying information rather than hide them.

6.5 From demonstrator to service
The goal is not to solve national interoperability in one prototype. It is to demonstrate that a focused clinical problem can be
improved by connecting the right information under explicit governance.
If useful, the same reusable modules can support vaccination continuity, chronic disease, medication safety, discharge
coordination, registries and other domains.

PART VII · From Assessment to Action
7.1 Assessment as the beginning of action
An assessment has value only if it reveals where improvement is possible. IML therefore links observed weaknesses to
practical actions, implementation priorities and opportunities for collaboration.

7.2 Support for low-resource ecosystems
Low-resource settings should not be judged against an architecture they cannot realistically deploy. The relevant question is
whether a safe, useful and maintainable progression path exists.
Open-source components, local deployment and offline capability may be especially valuable where connectivity and
procurement capacity are constrained.

7.3 High-resource fragmentation
High expenditure does not guarantee interoperability. Mature infrastructure can coexist with fragmented clinical
information, proprietary lock-in, delayed implementation and weak correction pathways.

7.4 The French paradox
France illustrates the possibility of advanced national digital-health infrastructure alongside persistent fragmentation,
implementation gaps and institutional difficulty in converting formal capability into consistently useful clinical information.
Authoritative audit evidence should therefore be incorporated into the Learning domain when it documents weak
evaluation, enforcement or follow-through.

7.5 Denmark as reference without idealisation
Denmark provides important references in microbiology, surveillance and national data infrastructures, including systems
such as MiBa, DANMAP and HAIBA. IML should use these as comparative resources without assuming that every
clinically relevant data element is always available in structured form at the required time.

7.6 Institutional responsiveness as implementation evidence
Institutions that publish strong strategies but cannot substantively answer documented implementation questions reveal a
maturity gap. Responsiveness is therefore not a courtesy metric; it is evidence about whether the ecosystem can learn.

7.7 Testing institutional follow-through through bounded tracer cases
For a bounded tracer, IML proposes reconstructing the chain from initial event or request to acknowledged receipt, allocated
responsibility, examination, substantive outcome, corrective action where indicated, and independently verifiable follow-up.
Absence of a public answer does not by itself establish misconduct or absent internal action; findings should state evidence
limitations explicitly.
The comparative research question is whether these direct observations add information beyond a high-level maturity
assessment, and under which conditions such pathway evidence generalises.

PART VIII · Open Clinical Workspace
8.1 Purpose
The Open Clinical Workspace is the implementation bridge between IML assessment and practical action. It is intended to
be open-source, modular, vendor-neutral, inspectable and progressively deployable.
It should be useful in primary care and other settings without requiring replacement of every existing application.

8.2 Not another monolithic EHR
The workspace should not be understood as a new national electronic health record. Existing hospital systems, laboratory
platforms, pharmacy applications and national infrastructures will continue to exist.
The workspace therefore emphasises interoperability, clinical context and reusable services rather than forced replacement.

8.3 Common clinical core
The reference architecture now organises the software around a common clinical core containing patient context, encounter
information, clinical history, terminology, results, audit, correction and provenance.
Clinical data should remain coherent independently of the national services or AI providers connected around it.

8.4 Open integration layer
Registries, laboratories, imaging services, identity systems, consent services and national digital-health platforms should
connect through explicit interfaces. This preserves the possibility of replacing a connector without rewriting the clinical
core.
Import may begin with structured files and progress toward APIs, FHIR resources, terminology services or event-based
messaging according to local maturity.

8.5 Country packs
Language, terminology, identity, reimbursement, messaging and regulation vary across countries. Country packs therefore
separate national adaptation from the common data and workflow model.

8.6 PostgreSQL reference foundation
PostgreSQL is the current reference relational data foundation for the implementation work. It provides a practical
environment for testing integrity, constraints, provenance, versioning, auditability, migration, backup and restoration.
This is a reference implementation choice, not a claim that one database technology is universally superior.

8.7 Local, remote and offline-capable deployment
The deployment strategy supports local or remote databases and APIs, with progressive synchronisation patterns and the
explicit goal of maintaining useful clinical operation when connectivity is weak or interrupted.
Reference deployment should remain multiplatform across macOS, Windows and Linux, with conservative Linux server
baselines and support for local clinical peripherals where required.

8.8 Terminology as a canonical service
Terminology is not a decorative coding layer. It is part of the clinical meaning of the record. The current implementation
consolidates a canonical iml_terminology environment with versioned concepts, designations, mappings, releases,
provenance and mapping-resolution structures.
KSH97-P provides a concise primary-care consultation vocabulary, while ICD-10 and national variants support more
extensive classification. Progressive mappings should remain explicit and reviewable.

8.9 Audit, correction and reuse
Meaningful actions should be attributable. Corrections should remain visible and reusable without silently rewriting the
historical record.
This architectural requirement directly supports the Learning domain, especially LRN-3.

8.10 AI as a replaceable service layer
AI functions should sit above durable clinical and evidence structures rather than become the structure itself. A model or
provider may change while the clinical record, terminology and evidence trail remain intact.
AI may assist summarisation, evidence retrieval, terminology support, missing-context detection and pattern recognition,
but the human professional remains responsible for interpretation and action.

8.11 From Reference Concept to Implementable Clinical Architecture
IML has begun to translate the Open Clinical Workspace from a conceptual reference environment into an implementable
software architecture. This development does not change the purpose of the workspace. It remains an open-source, vendor-
neutral and progressively deployable environment intended to support care, interoperability, evaluation and learning without
creating unnecessary dependency on a single software product, cloud provider or national platform.
The reference architecture is organised around three complementary layers: a common clinical core, an open integration
layer and country-specific adaptation packs. PostgreSQL provides the current reference data foundation, while terminology,
evidence retrieval and future AI services remain modular.
The deployment model is deliberately broader than a browser-only national portal. Local and remote operation, offline
capability, multiplatform deployment, exportability and documented dependencies are treated as resilience and autonomy
requirements.
These developments remain reference implementation work. Each module requires technical testing, clinical validation,
cybersecurity review, documentation, usability assessment and external scientific review before operational claims can be
made.

8.12 A Common Core with Replaceable Components
The Open Clinical Workspace should not become a new form of vendor lock-in created in the name of open source. Clinical
data structures, terminology, integration interfaces, evidence retrieval, analytics and AI services should be separated through
documented interfaces and explicit provenance.
A database engine, terminology service, AI model or external connector may evolve or be replaced without destroying the
meaning, history or auditability of the underlying health information.
This modularity is not merely a software-engineering preference. Reversibility, portability and the capacity to continue care
when a supplier, service or technology changes are part of the resilience of a Health Information Ecosystem.

PART IX · Responsible Artificial Intelligence and Scientific Integrity
9.1 Why responsible AI belongs in IML
AI now participates in the production, organisation, search, interpretation and communication of health information. It
therefore belongs inside the Health Information Ecosystem rather than outside it.
The relevant question is not whether AI exists, but under which conditions it can contribute without weakening clinical
responsibility or scientific integrity.

9.2 Human responsibility
AI outputs may support a professional judgement but should not obscure who is responsible for the decision. Systems
should identify when AI assistance occurred and preserve the information required for review.

9.3 Transparency and proportionality
The use of AI should be proportionate to the task and visible to users where relevant. Sensitive clinical information should
not be copied into external services merely because a model can process it.
Architecture should favour de-identification, minimum necessary data and separable service boundaries.

9.4 Bias, equity and representativeness
AI can reproduce biases present in data, access patterns, documentation practices and research publication. Evaluation
should therefore examine who is represented, who is missing and whether performance changes across contexts.
An AI system trained on a high-resource environment should not be assumed to transfer safely to a low-resource one.

9.5 Scientific integrity
AI-assisted research and writing should remain attributable and reproducible. Source selection, search logic, inclusion and
exclusion decisions, transformations and limitations should be documented sufficiently for review.
The existence of a fluent answer is not evidence of a reliable evidence process.

9.6 Traceable Clinical Evidence Retrieval · AI-EVIDENCE-01
One concrete AI workstream within the Open Clinical Workspace is a traceable clinical evidence-retrieval layer. Its purpose
is not to allow a generative model to produce apparently authoritative answers from opaque sources. Its purpose is to
preserve the chain between a clinical question, the evidence sources consulted, the records retrieved and the interpretation
subsequently presented to a professional.
The current reference architecture, identified as AI-EVIDENCE-01, separates evidence retrieval and provenance from later
AI-assisted interpretation. A query may be associated with a clinical encounter or diagnostic assessment while the external
search expression must remain de-identified. Query expressions and retrieval operations may be fingerprinted so that the
process can be audited without copying unnecessary patient-identifying information into external search workflows.
Each external source execution remains individually traceable. The architecture can record which source was consulted,
whether the request succeeded, returned no results, was partial, unavailable, rate-limited, failed or was cancelled, together
with result counts and technical metadata.
Retrieved publications or records are represented canonically and can retain identifiers such as PMID, PMCID, DOI, NCT
and Cochrane identifiers. The durable evidence record is separated from the transient AI model that may later summarise or
compare it.
The initial source registry includes PubMed/NCBI, Europe PMC, ClinicalTrials.gov, Crossref and public metadata from the
Cochrane Library. These sources serve different purposes: bibliographic discovery, open-access links, trial registration,
persistent metadata identification and systematic-review discovery.

Publisher full text is not stored by default. Access rights, licences and institutional entitlements remain respected.
Passwords, session cookies, API secrets and institutional credentials must not be stored in the evidence tables.
Connectors remain disabled until validated at application level. The existence of a schema or connector specification must
never be interpreted as evidence that a clinical AI service is operational, validated or safe.
AI may subsequently assist with summarisation, prioritisation, comparison, detection of missing context or presentation of
retrieved evidence. These functions should not erase provenance. A clinician or researcher should be able to determine
where information came from, what was searched, what was not retrieved, and what limitations were detected.
This architecture supports technological independence. Evidence provenance should survive replacement of an AI model or
provider. The model may change; the evidence trail should remain.

9.7 AI readiness as a maturity dimension
AI readiness should be assessed through information quality, governance, security, human oversight, traceability,
representativeness, workflow integration, correction and post-deployment learning. Procurement of an AI product is not
equivalent to readiness.

9.8 Boundaries of automation
IML should distinguish low-risk assistance from decisions with direct clinical consequences. The more consequential the
output, the stronger the requirement for validated data, explicit uncertainty, professional review, logging and the ability to
contest or correct the result.

Key Messages · Part IX
 • Clinical AI should preserve an auditable chain between the question, sources consulted, evidence retrieved and
   interpretation presented.
 • Evidence retrieval and provenance should be architecturally separated from generative model services.
 • External evidence connectors require explicit technical and clinical validation before operational use.
 • Clinical evidence queries sent to external services should be de-identified and proportionate to their purpose.
 • AI should help professionals examine evidence and uncertainty, not transform retrieval into an opaque source of
   authority.

PART X · Roadmap and Future Work
10.1 From manuscript to working programme
IML should continue to develop in parallel as a scientific framework, an assessment methodology, a reference
implementation and an open collaboration programme.
Progress should be visible through versioned documents, reproducible software migrations, country evidence profiles and
explicit validation status.

10.2 From specification to reference implementation
This transition has begun. Current development includes a PostgreSQL-oriented clinical architecture, canonical terminology
structures, a traceable clinical evidence-retrieval scaffold, an open integration strategy and country adaptation packs.
The terminology work is being consolidated around a canonical IML terminology environment so that classifications,
mappings and later revisions remain explicit and auditable. The AI evidence work establishes explicit structures for queries,
source executions, bibliographic records, identifiers and provenance before generative assistance is treated as a clinical
capability.
A reference implementation is not equivalent to a validated clinical product. Database migrations, software modules and
interfaces demonstrate architectural progress; they do not by themselves demonstrate clinical safety, effectiveness,
regulatory conformity or successful deployment.

D.3 Shared constructs and differences
Shared: governance, standards, financing, ethical safeguards, monitoring, continuity, workforce and technical infrastructure.
MEASURE explicitly includes financial audit (G-D2), monitoring influencing decisions (E-E1), and an interoperability
services layer. It should not be characterized as a technology-only framework.
Potential IML complement: preservation of clinical meaning, controlled correction and reuse, documentary reliability
(LRN-4), and institutional responsiveness/answerability (LRN-5). These concepts are present in the IML September 2026
manuscript; incremental validity remains untested.

D.4 Limitations and validation safeguards
      National/subnational maturity models cannot be validly scored from a single dispute or event.
      Crosswalk alignment does not establish equivalent scoring or predictive validity.
      Incomplete documentation must be labelled missing/unverified, not interpreted automatically as institutional failure.
      Independent review, sensitivity tests and traceable evidence are prerequisites for stronger claims.
      Historical literature search and forward citation tracking must continue before a comprehensive novelty claim.
