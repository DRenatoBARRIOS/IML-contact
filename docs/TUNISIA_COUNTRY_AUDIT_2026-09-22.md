# Tunisia IML Country Profile Audit

Assessment date: **22 September 2026**  
Documentary version: **0.1**  
Database version: **1**  
Publication state: **Exploratory / published on `main-test`**

## Decision

Tunisia has meaningful digital-health foundations and several operational services, but the public evidence does not support a claim of complete nationwide interoperability. Important components remain first phases, pilots, progressive roll-outs or funded future commitments.

The six equally weighted domain scores are:

| IML domain | Score | Evidence judgement |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Governance and standards | 62 | Current national direction, cross-government project tracking and major financed programmes; implementation results and a complete public architecture remain limited. |
| Technical interoperability | 55 | Operational Labes/CNAM exchange and first-phase national interoperability services; no public proof of nationwide semantic conformance or a complete standards implementation guide. |
| Identity and trust | 54 | E-Houwiya integration, legal consent and record-access rights, and a health-identifier rollout; nationwide assignment and operational consent/provenance controls remain unverified. |
| Adoption and clinical use | 58 | Several operational services and emergency tools; national denominators, active-use measures and longitudinal record completeness are not published. |
| Security and resilience | 52 | Mandatory national audit framework and health-data safeguards; health-sector audit coverage, incidents, continuity tests and recovery outcomes are insufficiently documented. |
| Feedback, correction and learning | 47 | Current emergency analytics, risk reporting duties and project monitoring are positive; public correction histories, data-quality closure, evaluation outcomes and institutional answerability remain weakly evidenced. |

**Overall orientation signal: 55/100**, calculated as the rounded arithmetic mean of the six equally weighted domain scores.

This is an evidence profile, not a certification or ranking.

## Scoring safeguards applied

- A policy, law, project approval or financed programme establishes documentary capacity but is not treated as proof of nationwide implementation.
- Pilot, first-phase and progressive-roll-out evidence receives partial rather than full implementation credit.
- Missing evidence is recorded as an unresolved question; it is not converted automatically into a zero.
- Operational claims are separated from forward-looking commitments.
- The WHO SCORE assessment is retained only as a dated historical baseline using 2013–2018 data and is not presented as Tunisia's current 2026 position.
- Every indicator claim includes a limitation. Public links are separated from documentary URLs and are governed by the semantic source manifest.

## Domain findings

### 1. Governance and standards — 62

The strongest evidence is the ongoing roll-out of a named National Digital Health Transformation strategy, WHO implementation support, the Tunisia Health System Strengthening Project and centralized government monitoring of digital projects. These show institutional direction, financing and coordination.

The score is capped because the reviewed sources do not publish a complete current enterprise architecture, implementation timetable with achieved results, national conformance programme or independent evaluation of digital-health delivery.

### 2. Technical interoperability — 55

Labes/CNAM provides quantified evidence of operational exchange, while the Government reports a first phase of the national interoperability platform and social-sector interoperability services. Sahetna.tn, the National Health Identifier and planned electronic records add credible implementation activity.

The score does not assume nationwide interoperability. No public national FHIR implementation guide, shared clinical terminology service, exchange-profile conformance results, message success rates or comprehensive public/private coverage evidence was found.

### 3. Identity and trust — 54

The national digital identity is integrated with E-CNAM. Law No. 2024-32 establishes traceable consent, medical-record privacy and access rights. A health-specific identifier is in progressive roll-out.

The remaining gap is operational proof: national assignment was not demonstrated as complete, and the review found insufficient public evidence on patient matching quality, granular authorization, consent portability, provenance and correction across health applications.

### 4. Adoption and clinical use — 58

The evidence register includes operational Labes exchanges, E-CNAM citizen services, first-phase digital-hospital and Najda services, and WHO-documented emergency information tools. These prevent the profile from treating Tunisia as a policy-only environment.

The score remains below 60 because national coverage denominators, active-user rates, routine clinical workflow measures, private-provider participation and longitudinal completeness are not available in the reviewed public evidence.

### 5. Security and resilience — 52

Tunisia has a current ANCS audit framework covering public information systems, certified auditors and follow-up of audit recommendations. Health-record privacy is protected by statute, and INPDP has health-sector-specific guidance.

Formal controls are stronger than the published operational evidence. No representative health-sector audit results, remediation closure rates, incident series, ransomware impact analysis, backup restoration results or continuity-test outcomes were found.

### 6. Feedback, correction and learning — 47

Positive evidence includes WHO-documented use of real-time information in outbreak response, government project monitoring, statutory quality/risk units and adverse-event reporting.

The score is limited by the absence of public correction logs, version histories, response-time datasets, substantive-answer measures and evidence that findings consistently change policy or care. The detailed WHO SCORE assessment is old but identifies the questions that a current reassessment must answer: facility-data quality checks, reporting completeness, analytical review, public data access and a national monitoring-and-evaluation architecture.

For **LRN-5 institutional responsiveness**, the 2024 law requires suggestion and complaint channels and responses within a reasonable period. This establishes a duty, not demonstrated performance. LRN-5 is therefore **not assessed**, rather than scored zero, pending representative evidence of receipt, substantive answers and corrective follow-through.

## Priority follow-up evidence

1. National denominator and active-use data for Sahetna.tn, the National Health Identifier, electronic records, Najda and hospital systems.
2. Published interoperability architecture, data standards, terminology governance and conformance-test results.
3. Health-sector cybersecurity audit coverage, unresolved findings, incidents, continuity exercises and recovery performance.
4. Facility-data completeness and quality indicators, with correction closure and version history.
5. Complaint and information-request response times, substantive-answer rates and evidence of corrective action for LRN-5.
6. Independent outcome evaluation separating successful pilots from national-scale delivery.

## Source-quality status

The evidence register contains twelve sources from Tunisian public bodies, WHO, the World Bank, AFD, the Council of Europe/INPDP and a clearly labelled secondary report of a Ministry announcement. The WHO UHC link uses the live-monitoring route with the explicit country parameter `iso3=TUN`; generic landing routes that require a second country selection are rejected. Two documentary sources are intentionally not exposed as public buttons: the Ministry of Social Affairs page pending stable revalidation, and the WHO SCORE PDF because the current semantic auditor cannot reliably inspect compressed PDF text. Their documentary URLs, roles and limitations remain recorded.
